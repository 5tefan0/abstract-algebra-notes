# Lecture 7 — Quotient Groups and the First Isomorphism Theorem

## 1. Review: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as a Quotient

Recall from Lecture 6: the subgroups of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ are exactly the sets $n\mathbb{Z} = \{kn : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ for $n \geq 0$. From each such subgroup $n\mathbb{Z}$ we constructed a new group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the **quotient group**, whose elements are the residue classes (cosets of $n\mathbb{Z}$):

$$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \{ \bar{0}, \bar{1}, \ldots, \overline{n-1} \}, \quad \bar{a} + \bar{b} = \overline{a+b}.$$

This is a cyclic group of order $n$ generated by $\bar{1}$. There is a natural surjective homomorphism
$$\pi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, \quad a \mapsto \bar{a},$$
with $\ker(\pi) = n\mathbb{Z}$. This construction — first written down by Gauss in his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801), from the Latin *modulus* — is the prototype for the general quotient group.

**Remark (Rings).** The integers also carry a multiplication, making them a **ring**: a set that is an abelian group under addition and has an associative multiplication with identity $1$ that distributes over addition. The same multiplication descends to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (by $\bar{a} \cdot \bar{b} = \overline{ab}$), making it a finite ring. The **unit group** $R^\times$ of a ring $R$ is the set of elements with multiplicative inverses; it forms a group under multiplication. For $R = \mathbb{Z}$, the unit group is $\{+1, -1\}$.

## 2. When Does a Quotient Group Exist?

Given a group $G$ and a subgroup $H \leq G$, we ask: when can the set of left cosets

$$G/H = \{ aH : a \in G \}$$

be given a group structure via the **naive multiplication law**

$$(aH)(bH) \stackrel{?}{=} (ab)H?$$

We analyze three cases.

### Case 1: $H = \ker(f)$ for a Homomorphism $f: G \to G'$

The cosets of $H = \ker(f)$ are exactly the **fibers** of $f$: for each element $f(a) \in \mathrm{Im}(f)$, the fiber $f^{-1}(f(a))$ is the coset $aH$. This gives a bijection

$$G/H \longleftrightarrow \mathrm{Im}(f) \subseteq G'.$$

Since $\mathrm{Im}(f)$ is a subgroup of $G'$, it carries a group structure. We **transport** this structure to $G/H$: the product of cosets $aH$ and $bH$ corresponds to $f(a) \cdot f(b) = f(ab)$, hence to the coset $abH$. So

$$(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$$

is well-defined, and $G/H$ is a group isomorphic to $\mathrm{Im}(f)$.

### Case 2: An Arbitrary Subgroup — Naive Multiplication Can Fail

Now let $H \leq G$ be any subgroup (not necessarily a kernel). Attempt to define $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$.

**Problem.** A coset has many representations: if $aH = a'H$ (i.e., $a'^{-1}a \in H$) and $bH = b'H$, we need $(ab)H = (a'b')H$. This requires $a'b'(ab)^{-1} \in H$, which is not guaranteed in general.

**Concrete failure.** Suppose there exists $a \in G$ and $h \in H$ such that $aha^{-1} \notin H$. Consider the product $(aH)(a^{-1}H)$: the formula gives $(aa^{-1})H = H$, the identity coset. But within $aH$ we have the element $ah$, and within $a^{-1}H$ we have $a^{-1}$. Their product is $aha^{-1}$, which lies outside $H$ by hypothesis — contradicting the requirement that all products from $aH \times a^{-1}H$ land in $H$. So the multiplication is **not well-defined**.

### Case 3: $H$ Normal in $G$ — Naive Multiplication Works

**Definition.** A subgroup $H \leq G$ is **normal**, written $H \trianglelefteq G$, if

$$aHa^{-1} = H \quad \text{for all } a \in G,$$

equivalently, $aH = Ha$ as sets for every $a \in G$.

**Theorem.** If $H \trianglelefteq G$, then the naive multiplication law on cosets is well-defined and makes $G/H$ into a group.

**Proof (well-definedness).** We show that as sets, $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$, where the left side denotes all products $\{(ah)(bh') : h, h' \in H\}$.

$$\begin{aligned}
(aH)(bH) &= \{ah \cdot bh' : h, h' \in H\} \\
&= a(Hb)H \quad \text{(regroup factors)} \\
&= a(bH)H \quad \text{(since } Hb = bH \text{ by normality of }H\text{)} \\
&= (ab)(HH) \quad \text{(associativity)} \\
&= (ab)H. \quad \text{(since }H \cdot H = H\text{ as }H\text{ is a subgroup)}
\end{aligned}$$

Here the key step is using normality to rewrite $Hb = bH$, which allows us to slide $b$ past $H$.

**Group structure on $G/H$:**
- **Multiplication:** $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$.
- **Identity:** $H = eH$ (the coset containing $e$).
- **Inverse:** $(aH)^{-1} = a^{-1}H$.
- **Associativity:** inherited from $G$: $((aH)(bH))(cH) = (ab)H \cdot cH = (abc)H = aH \cdot (bc)H = (aH)((bH)(cH))$. $\square$

**Converse.** If the naive multiplication on $G/H$ is well-defined, then $H$ must be normal (the argument in Case 2 shows the failure when normality fails). So:

> The naive multiplication on cosets defines a group structure on $G/H$ **if and only if** $H \trianglelefteq G$.

## 3. The Quotient Group and the Natural Homomorphism

**Definition.** When $H \trianglelefteq G$, the group $(G/H, \cdot)$ constructed above is called the **quotient group** of $G$ by $H$.

**Proposition.** There is a natural surjective group homomorphism

$$\pi: G \to G/H, \quad a \mapsto aH,$$

with $\ker(\pi) = H$.

**Proof.** $\pi$ is a homomorphism: $\pi(ab) = (ab)H = (aH)(bH) = \pi(a)\pi(b)$. It is surjective since every coset $aH$ is the image of $a$. The kernel is $\{a \in G : aH = H\} = H$. $\square$

**Corollary.** Every normal subgroup $H \trianglelefteq G$ is the kernel of a surjective group homomorphism (namely $\pi: G \to G/H$). Combined with the fact that kernels are always normal (Lecture 4), we obtain:

$$H \trianglelefteq G \iff H = \ker(f) \text{ for some group homomorphism } f \text{ from } G.$$

## 4. The First Isomorphism Theorem

**Theorem (First Isomorphism Theorem).** Let $f: G \to G'$ be a surjective group homomorphism with kernel $H = \ker(f)$. Then $f$ induces an isomorphism

$$\bar{f}: G/H \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} G', \quad \bar{f}(aH) = f(a).$$

**Proof.**

*Well-defined:* If $aH = a'H$, then $a'^{-1}a \in H = \ker(f)$, so $f(a'^{-1}a) = e'$, giving $f(a) = f(a')$. Thus $\bar{f}(aH) = f(a)$ is independent of the coset representative.

*Homomorphism:* $\bar{f}((aH)(bH)) = \bar{f}(abH) = f(ab) = f(a)f(b) = \bar{f}(aH)\bar{f}(bH)$.

*Surjective:* $f$ is surjective, so for any $g' \in G'$, $\exists\, a \in G$ with $f(a) = g'$, hence $\bar{f}(aH) = g'$.

*Injective:* Suppose $\bar{f}(aH) = e'$, i.e., $f(a) = e'$. Then $a \in H$, so $aH = H = e_{G/H}$. Thus $\ker(\bar{f}) = \{e_{G/H}\}$. $\square$

**Diagram.** Any surjective homomorphism $f: G \twoheadrightarrow G'$ with kernel $H$ factors as

$$G \xrightarrow{\;\pi\;} G/H \xrightarrow{\;\bar{f}\;} G',$$

where $\pi$ is the natural projection and $\bar{f}$ is an isomorphism. More generally, any homomorphism $f: G \to G'$ (not necessarily surjective) factors through the quotient by its kernel: $G \to G/\ker(f) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathrm{Im}(f) \hookrightarrow G'$.

## 5. Short Exact Sequences

**Definition.** A **short exact sequence of groups** is a diagram

$$1 \to H \xrightarrow{\;g\;} G \xrightarrow{\;f\;} G' \to 1$$

where all maps are group homomorphisms, $g$ is injective, $f$ is surjective, and $\mathrm{Im}(g) = \ker(f)$ (exactness at $G$). Here $1$ denotes the trivial group.

In other words: $g$ identifies $H$ with a normal subgroup of $G$, and $f$ exhibits $G'$ as the quotient $G/H$. By the First Isomorphism Theorem, $G' \cong G/\ker(f) = G/\mathrm{Im}(g) \cong G/H$.

**Interpretation.** A short exact sequence says the group $G$ is "built from" $H$ (the kernel/subgroup) and $G'$ (the quotient). The group $G$ is called an **extension** of $G'$ by $H$.

## 6. Warning: Extensions Are Not Unique

Knowing $H$ and $G'$ does **not** determine $G$.

**Example.** Both $S_3$ and $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ fit into short exact sequences with $H \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and $G' \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$:

$$1 \to A_3 \to S_3 \xrightarrow{\mathrm{sgn}} \{+1,-1\} \to 1,$$

$$1 \to \langle \bar{2} \rangle \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{a \mapsto a \bmod 2} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 1.$$

In the first sequence, $A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic of order 3) and the quotient is $\{+1,-1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

In the second sequence, the kernel $\langle \bar{2} \rangle = \{\bar{0}, \bar{2}, \bar{4}\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and the quotient is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

Both sequences have isomorphic ends—$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$—yet the groups in the middle are non-isomorphic: $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian of order 6, while $S_3$ is non-abelian of order 6. The extra data required to specify $G$ from $H$ and $G'$—how $G'$ acts on $H$ via conjugation—is the **extension problem**, a central topic in group theory.
